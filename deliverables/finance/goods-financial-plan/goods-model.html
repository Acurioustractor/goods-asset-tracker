Goods on Country · the model, start to finish · 9 September 2026

Reroute existing spend through a community-owned channel

Goods on Country is a model for moving manufacturing and economic ownership into communities, with beds as the first proven product. This page tells it in order: why the model is shaped this way and who says so, what one bed does, where last year's money went, what a bed costs and earns, what Goods sells whether or not a funder says yes, every way a bed gets paid for, what Witta can make, the cash month by month to July 2027, what a loan is good for, and the asks. The last section is live, so any number here can be changed and the year redrawn.

1 · Mission and focus

How a bed becomes a community enterprise

The output of this work is community-owned enterprises. Beds are the first product that proves one can be stood up. Map the need. Find the institutional buyers who already hold budgets for health and housing outcomes. Connect them to a community enterprise that is selling. That reroutes existing spend through a community-owned channel, and each deployment teaches the next one about need, buyers, making and governance.

“Success is measured by community enterprises operating and local revenue retained, not beds produced.” Ben Knight, 9 September 2026

2 · Impact

Four outcomes, counted per bed

The four outcomes the funders keep asking about are employment, community impact, recycling and health, with economic ownership running through all of them. They are counted per bed, so every ask can be read the same way: this much money buys this many beds, and each bed carries the same outcomes. Scabies and rheumatic heart disease are why a bed off the floor matters. Nothing here claims a health result that has not been measured. The figures below are for this year's plan of 413 funded beds. 4 · The cost model, simply

One bed carries the business

A bed sells for $750 at the factory door. $276 makes it, with the person on the press inside that number, so the making is a wage from the first bed. $474 stays with Goods for rent, the founders and everything else. Freight is charged on top at cost, per community, because Utopia and Maningrida do not cost the same to reach. The business is never priced cost-plus and the organisation is never divided by beds to make a bed look dear. This is the number that let today's "half a million to raise" be retired: the running costs are carried by what stays in every bed, plus what else Goods sells.

7 · Making

What Witta holds, what it can make, when the plants must be live

The count at Witta on 9 September: 812 tabs, 650 leg pieces, about 59 uncut tab sheets, 145 kg of weighed shred and roughly 1,150 kg more in buckets, offcuts and router skeletons. Legs and shred are the limit. There are more tabs on the shelf than either. Witta presses six sheets a day, two to a bed: 60 beds a month on its own panels, 120 if Defy supplies the leg panels. With three plants coming on through 2027 the plan does not run short; the question is shred supply and when the second press goes in, not whether the beds can be made.

8 · The year to July 2027

Every dollar in and out, month by month

The year read as cash in the bank, month by month. ALIVE's $75,000 for a hundred beds is already banked, so those beds leave stock in November with the $474 a bed already earned. Grants land after their boards, which is why December matters. Buyers who pay a community enterprise are not here, by design. The chart is driven by the controls in section 11, so a later Snow decision or a freight rule can be tried before it is promised.

Two things this chart does on purpose, and they both make it look worse than the year probably is. Goods makes 713 beds and is paid for 510. Of the 203 nobody pays for, about 120 are first-stock beds whose funder has not said yes yet, which is the catalytic gap, and about 83 are made in July as stock for the year after, which is inventory. And freight is set to nothing recovered, so Goods carries all $71,300 of it. The price model says freight is charged on top at cost, so set freight recovered to 100 in section 11 to see the year the way it is meant to be priced.

9 · Community impact investing

What a loan is good for, and the shape of ten years

Repayable capital only works where the beds it buys bring cash back. A plant does: its first year of paid beds covers the service on a loan that built it, and the plant is the asset that ends up community-owned. Gifted first stock does not: the community keeps the sale, so there is nothing to repay from. That is the line to hold with Sefa and with the Paul Ramsay and Atlassian fund, and the reason production capital and community enterprise capital are asked for separately. The ten-year chart underneath shows where the beds get made, which is the claim the whole model rests on.

10 · The asks

One sentence per funder, four outcomes each

Every funder gets the same sentence with their own numbers in it, and the table below is that sentence in columns. Snow and Dusseldorp are asked for community enterprise capital. Brian M. Davis is asked for beds and for the facilitation that stands an enterprise up. QBE is asked for production capital. Tim Fairfax is asked for operating support, which is the one ask that is not counted in beds. Sefa is repayable and belongs against a plant. Nobody is asked to fund a gap.

The money kept locally column is the full sale value of the beds the enterprise holds, before whatever it costs that enterprise to sell them. It is what the community controls. What it clears depends on its own cost of selling.

The live total does not add up to a bed count, and it should not. Of it, first stock is the only part that buys beds; the rest builds plants, pays for running the business or has to be repaid. Minderoo is listed because the conversation may revive, and left out of the total because it has not. Amounts for Snow, Dusseldorp and others are ours until an ask exists.
