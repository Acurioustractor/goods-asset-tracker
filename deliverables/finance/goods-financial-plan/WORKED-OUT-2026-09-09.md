## The five-layer model (Ben, 9 Sep 2026, from the transcript)

Goods on Country is a model for transferring manufacturing and economic ownership into communities, using beds as the first proven product. The goal is not to produce beds forever; it is to produce community-owned enterprises.

1. **Community demand.** Map need and buyers.
2. **Catalytic enterprise capital.** Witta-made beds become the first trading stock of a community enterprise. Philanthropy pays Goods $750 a bed for that stock.
3. **Institutional demand.** Buyers who already have budgets for health and housing outcomes (Centrecorp, NT Health and Miwatj, Shepherdson College, Ceduna Aboriginal Corporation, ALIVE) buy beds from the community enterprise. Existing spend is rerouted through a community-owned channel. The money stays with the community.
4. **Manufacturing transfer.** Once the enterprise muscle exists, invest in local production. QBE's plants. Alice Springs through DEWR.
5. **Shared services and governance.** Indigenous-led shared capability across communities: procurement, design, finance, training, story.

Capital is aligned to jobs: employment capital, production capital, community enterprise capital, public-good capital. Each dollar has a job. Success is measured by community enterprises operating and local revenue retained, not beds produced. Each deployment teaches the next.

What this changes in the money: buyers are never catalytic capital. ALIVE is an institutional buyer whose 100 beds ($75,000, paid in July) are a separate order delivered around Central Australia under ALIVE's own grant, outside the 400 entirely. The 400 first-stock beds are paid for by catalytic capital: Tim Fairfax 133, Brian M. Davis 80, and 187 beds ($140,000) still to find from philanthropy, or a smaller first year of 213. Institutional buyers then pay the community enterprise for the beds it sells. Named money for Goods this year: $500,000 (TFFF $100K, BMD $100K, QBE $300K).

After the deck: the story scroll on the site (the narrative spine, built from consented Empathy Ledger voices such as Karen Little, Kristy Bloomfield and the Ntjillaburra story, doing design work not just emotional work), then the website as four journeys: I'm a community, I'm a buyer, I'm a funder, I'm a supporter.

One answer: **a bed costs $276 to make at Witta, and that includes $80 of paid factory labour.** Freight to where it goes is charged on top of the $750, at cost, as its own line per community, about $100 to $150 a bed (Ben, 9 Sep: freight on top). The make cost does not include Nic, Ben, rent or the office. There is no coordinator salary: the person on the press is the $80 (Ben, 9 Sep). Those are the cost of running the business, section 2.

What that means at $750 at the factory door: **$474 of every bed stays in Goods on Country** to pay rent, founders and everything else. Freight is on top and paid by whoever buys the bed, so it never comes out of the $474. Not profit; contribution.

## 5. What we need

Under the price model there is no raise for "the organisation". The organisation is paid by the whole business, which last year made $1.46M and kept $260K. What the raise buys is new work:

| New work this year | Cost | Paid by |
|---|---:|---|
| 400 beds at $750 | $300,000 | ALIVE (paid), Tim Fairfax, Brian M. Davis, other buyers |
| Facilitation, four communities | $40,000 | Brian M. Davis |
| Two community plants | $300,000 | QBE |
| **Named money** | **$640,000** | |

If QBE, Tim Fairfax and Brian M. Davis all land, the year's new work is fully paid and nothing else is needed. If one falls over, the bed count falls with it and the plants wait; the business still runs.

The one thing to watch is not a raise. It is that the business's other income holds up: last year Snow alone was $375,000 of Goods' $653,000 and there is no current Snow ask. That is the real gap in the year, and it is a relationship, not a spreadsheet line.

## 6. Where I went wrong today

1. I took Matt's annual provisions for the factory and the organisation and treated them as programme costs the grants had to fund. They are the cost of being Goods, paid by the whole business.
2. I then reported the difference, "$503,846 still to raise", as money to find. It was never a raise.
3. I spread the whole organisation over beds alone to make a cost per bed. Beds are one of the things Goods sells; the same people also make washers, run workshops and build containers. That produced "$1,495 a bed", which is meaningless.
4. I called the $426 "materials and freight" when it already includes $80 of paid factory labour and $150 of freight. The make cost is $276 at the factory door; freight is its own line after; what neither includes is the business.
