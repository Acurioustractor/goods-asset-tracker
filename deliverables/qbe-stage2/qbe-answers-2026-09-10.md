## Q6. Proposed use of funds

$300,000 builds two production plants in communities that have asked for one.

A community does not buy a facility. It chooses modules in feed order on top of a site base, and
each module has a supplier behind it.

| Module | Low | High | Where the price comes from |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Site base: 40ft and 20ft containers, genset, crane, electrical, ventilation, pad, PPE | $31,800 | $64,000 | Modelled. Crane and containers quoted. |
| Collection and sorting | $5,000 | $19,500 | Estimated |
| Shredding | $19,800 | $19,800 | Telford Smith |
| Pressing, CNC and finishing | $32,780 | $32,780 | Circularity bundle |
| Assembly and workshop | $6,387 | $6,387 | Carbatec |
| All modules and the site base | $95,767 | $142,967 | |

The $150,000 we have allowed is an allowance, not a quote. It sits above the high column so that
a site with a harder pad, a longer power run or a container that has to be barged does not stop
the build.

A plant goes where a community has 200 beds a year of recorded need and a local operator to run
it. Five communities have asked us for their own plant: Palm Island, Alice Springs, Maningrida,
Utopia and Tennant Creek. Alice Springs is already funded through Oonchiumpa's Commonwealth
money and is outside this request. The two we plan to build with this grant are Palm Island,
first, and Maningrida.

A plant takes about three months from money to first bed. It starts at 200 beds a year on a
single press and can reach 720 on the same equipment as the local crew builds speed. Maningrida
has already made beds with us: 40 pressed at our facility, sent north, and assembled at Gamardi
by young people with Homeland School Company. Palm Island's council, the community and PICC have
each asked for a facility.

This grant does not buy beds. The first stock is paid for separately, by the Tim Fairfax Family
Foundation and the Brian M. Davis Charitable Foundation, so that a community's plant and a
community's stock are never competing for the same dollar. Keeping them apart is what lets a
plant be judged on whether it runs.

What the money produces that we can count: two plants standing and pressing; the beds they make
in their first year; the hours of paid local work that go into them; and the plastic that goes
through the shredder instead of the tip. At month six we ask four questions at each site that
have a yes or a no. Who holds the keys. Who runs the payroll. Who invoices the buyer. Whether at
least half the production is local.

## Q8. How the funded activity touches related entities

No related entity delivers any part of the funded activity. The applicant builds both plants and
buys every module from a supplier at arm's length. What follows is every related-party connection
a reader should know about, including the ones this grant does not touch.

### The entities

The Butterfly Movement Ltd is the applicant and trades as Goods on Country. A Curious Tractor Pty
Ltd is related through the founders and has been the operating home for parts of this work during
the transition. A Kind Tractor Ltd is dormant and carries no activity.

### Oonchiumpa, and the connection due diligence would find

Oonchiumpa is an Aboriginal community-controlled organisation in Alice Springs. Kristy Bloomfield
is a director of Oonchiumpa and a director of the applicant. She also leads the Oonchiumpa
relationship for Goods on Country.

Oonchiumpa holds a Commonwealth offer from the Department of Employment and Workplace Relations
for $1,695,000 over four years, dated 12 August 2026, with the agreement still to be executed.
Under it Oonchiumpa expects to pay Goods on Country about $150,000 to build a production plant in
Alice Springs. A further amount of up to $150,000 for plant equipment is in train with the
National Indigenous Australians Agency, where no invitation has yet been issued.

That plant and that money sit outside this request. The two plants QBE would fund are in other
communities. In Alice Springs, Goods on Country is a builder paid to do a job by the organisation
that holds the funding, and the money is spent on the build.

Kristy declares her interest at both boards and takes no part in a decision of either board about
a transaction between them. Oonchiumpa's own decision to commission the plant is made by its board
without her.

### The founders' entities, and the transfer under way

Part of the Goods trading history sits in a founder's sole trader ledger, because that is where
the work was done before the applicant took it on. Our accountants, Standard Ledger, are
documenting the FY26 carve-out into the applicant.

The production equipment at our Witta facility was bought by the founders through the sole trader
and through A Curious Tractor, and is being transferred into the applicant. No related entity is
paid for the use of it, then or now.

### Who is involved and is not a related entity

Community partners assemble, deliver and make the local decisions, and they benefit directly by
design, which is the purpose of the programme. Defy Design, a Sydney recycled-plastic
manufacturer, supplies bed kits and shredded plastic as an ordinary supplier at commercial rates.

No related entity benefits from this grant, directly or indirectly, beyond the transfer of assets
into the applicant that is already under way.

## Q18. How QBE funding would be catalytic

A QBE decision releases money that is already invited and cannot be paid without it.

The Brian M. Davis Charitable Foundation invited us on 1 September to apply for up to $100,000.
Their budget template sets the condition in writing:

> Please note BMDCF only pays out a grant once all funding for a project has been confirmed. If an
> application to another funding body is unsuccessful, or other income becomes unavailable, you
> must advise BMDCF as soon as possible.

Our budget names QBE as the other funding body. So their money moves when yours does, and it stays
still when yours does not. The dates line up in that order:

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| 25 September | This application and the Brian M. Davis application both close |
| 6 and 7 October | QBE interviews |
| 23 October | QBE conditional outcomes |
| October | Brian M. Davis grants committee |
| 19 November | Brian M. Davis board decides |
| Late November | Tim Fairfax Family Foundation decides |

A conditional outcome on 23 October is in our hands almost four weeks before the Brian M. Davis
board sits. That is the whole mechanism, and it is the reason we are applying to you for the
plants and to them for the beds and the youth facilitation.

The second thing your money unlocks takes longer to arrive and is worth more. Nobody will lend
against a community-made bed today, because nobody knows what one costs to make in a community.
Our costs come from a shed in Witta. The two plants this grant builds produce that number in their
first year, weighed and counted at the site, alongside the operating cost of running a plant a
long way from a city. We are in conversation with Sefa and with the Paul Ramsay Foundation and
Atlassian Foundation's Impact First Fund about repayable capital. Neither conversation can become
an application until the number exists. Our own test says a $200,000 loan needs 101 paid beds a
year to service it, which is a plant's output and not a guess about demand. Grant capital builds
plants one and two. Measured cost is what lets debt build plants three and four, and that is how
this stops needing grants.

One thing here is a question for you. Your preconditions date is 13 November. The
Brian M. Davis board sits on 19 November and Tim Fairfax decides late in the same month. Both are
invitations we received in the last fortnight and neither board can be moved. We would like to
know whether a conditional board outcome, or the invitation itself, can stand as the confirmation
you need on 13 November.
