## The one strategic decision, before any words are written

Their budget template, sheet INSTRUCTIONS cell B16, says: **"BMDCF only pays out a grant once all
funding for a project has been confirmed."** That condition is in the template and nowhere in the
Grant Conditions PDF, so it is easy to miss and it decides the shape of this application.

**Scope the project to exactly the $100,000.** If the project is "80 beds and facilitation in four
communities", then all funding for the project is this grant alone, and the condition is satisfied
the moment they approve it. If the project is written as part of the $747,950 year, then payment
waits on QBE, Tim Fairfax and Snow, and the money may not move until 2027.

The plants, the 400 beds and the organisation stay out of this application. They belong to QBE and
Tim Fairfax. This one is self-contained on purpose.

## ABOUT THE ORGANISATION

### Organisation history, vision and mission, max 250 words

Goods on Country makes things communities have asked for, in the communities that asked. It is a
registered business name of The Butterfly Movement Ltd, a charity governed by three Indigenous
directors: Kristy Bloomfield, Audrey Deemal and Jeremy Donovan.

The work started with a bed. In remote communities, families sleep on floors because a bed cannot be
bought locally, and freight costs more than the bed. We designed a bed with communities that answers
that: the Stretch Bed sits off the ground, the canvas comes off and washes, every part can be
replaced, and the legs are pressed from 20 kg of recycled plastic. It assembles in about five
minutes with no tools.

540 beds are now deployed across eleven communities. 320 have been bought and paid for by four
organisations for $273,966, including 100 paid for up front by the ALIVE National Centre at the
University of Melbourne in August 2026. Forty were pressed at our own facility in Queensland and
assembled in Maningrida by local workers.

The vision is not a bed company. It is that the making, the selling and the money stay in community.
Our model puts first trading stock into the hands of a community enterprise, connects it to buyers
who already hold health and housing budgets, and moves the manufacturing plant itself into community
hands once the enterprise can run it. The enterprise sells the beds and keeps the full sale price.

We do not claim a health outcome. We claim a person off the floor, a paid hour of work, and a
kilogram of plastic kept out of landfill, and we count each of them against a record.

### Primary beneficiaries, max 150 words

Young people in remote Aboriginal communities who want paid work where they live, and the households
who receive the beds they make and deliver.

The young people are the reason this application exists. In Maningrida, local young workers assembled
forty beds at Gamardi. In Alice Springs and Utopia, young people working with an aged care service
made more than twenty beds for Elders. Karen Liddle, who leads that work, put it this way: "To see
kids' faces with joy after making a bed, it just really hits you." Mykel, a young man at Utopia,
said after a build day: "Yeah, I'll be rocking up every day to make them."

The households are the second beneficiary. They are families in very remote communities where
overcrowding runs at 49% in Central Australia and 70% in Arnhem Land, against 15% nationally.

### Initiative description, max 300 words

This grant does two things, and they fit together.

$60,000 buys 80 Stretch Beds at $750, the published price every buyer pays. Those 80 beds become the
first trading stock of community enterprises in four communities. The enterprise sells them to buyers
who already hold health and housing budgets, and keeps the money. Nothing returns to Goods. That is
the point: a community enterprise with stock has something to sell, and something to make more of.

$40,000 pays for the work around it in four communities at $10,000 each: the trips, the build days,
the training, the delivery, and the slow work of a local organisation deciding how it wants to run.
We have billed and been paid at this rate before, on a real invoice, so the number is proven.

Young people are paid to make and deliver. A bed takes about two hours of paid making, and $80 of
every $750 is the maker's wage. Across 80 beds that is 160 paid hours going to young people in their
own community, alongside the delivery and assembly work.

The communities are ones we already work in, where beds have already arrived and relationships
already exist: Palm Island, Maningrida, Utopia and Tennant Creek are the working choices. No
community is named in a funding application before it has agreed, so the final four are confirmed
with each organisation before the grant starts.

Every bed carries 20 kg of recycled plastic. Across 80 beds that is 1.6 tonnes kept out of landfill,
in places that sit outside kerbside collection entirely.

### End goals, two to three, max 120 words

**One.** Eighty beds are made and delivered, with young people paid for the making and the delivery,
and every bed traceable to a household, a community and a date.

**Two.** Four community organisations hold first trading stock and complete at least one sale each,
keeping the full sale price locally.

**Three.** 1.6 tonnes of plastic is diverted from landfill and pressed into a product that stays in
use for years.

### Why is this initiative needed, max 300 words

A child sleeping on a floor in an overcrowded house is the start of a chain that ends in heart
surgery. Skin infection leads to acute rheumatic fever, and repeated episodes cause rheumatic heart
disease. The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare names household overcrowding as the risk
factor and defines it by the Canadian National Occupancy Standard.

The numbers are not marginal. Rheumatic heart disease among First Nations people in the Northern
Territory runs at 3,398.7 per 100,000. In 2024 there were 506 new acute rheumatic fever diagnoses in
Australia, 93% of them First Nations, and 211 of them in children aged five to fourteen. Overcrowding
by that same standard runs at 70% in Arnhem Land and 49% in Central Australia against 15%
nationally.

You cannot buy a bed in these communities. One provider in Alice Springs sells $3 million a year of
washing machines into remote communities, and most of them are in the dump within months. Daniel Patrick
Noble, on Palm Island, described why: "the cost of, not only purchasing it, the cost of having it
freighted over as well, it all adds." Mark, also on Palm Island, described what people do instead:
"We put together crates, tied them up with plastic, joined them together to make it look like a bed."

The second need is work. In very remote Australia 35% of First Nations people aged 25 to 64 are
employed, against 62% in major cities. Young people leave because there is nothing to stay for.

The third is waste. 87% of Australia's plastic goes straight to landfill, and remote communities sit
outside kerbside collection altogether. The same plastic that has nowhere to go is the material the
bed is made from.

The project is scoped so that this grant is its entire funding. No part of it waits on another

### National Principles for Child Safe Organisations

**NEEDS BEN, and this one is a real gap.** The policies register records a child safety process at
**draft**, with a consultation underway with Fred Campbell. A youth funder will read this answer
closely, and overstating it would be worse than naming it.

The honest answer names three things: the draft process and its consultation and completion date;
that the work happens inside community organisations that hold their own child safe policies and
screening, with those named; and what upholding children's rights looks like in practice, which is
that young people are paid for their work at a named rate, their images and words are used only with
recorded consent, and their community organisation decides what happens locally.

Do not submit this question until the draft is finished or the date is set.

### Key risks and mitigations, max 250 words

**A community has not agreed.** We will not name a community in a grant application before it has
agreed to the work. Four are our working choices and each confirms before the grant starts. If one
declines, the stock moves to another community where beds have already been delivered.

**The making bottleneck.** Our press makes three beds a day, which is the slowest step. At three a day across sixteen run days a month, eighty beds
is under two months of press time and sits comfortably inside a year. A second press is being priced
and is not required for this project.

**Freight.** Remote freight is $150 a bed all up, $100 to the factory and $50 to community, measured
on a real forty-bed run to Maningrida. It is costed into the delivery and does not come out of the
wage.

**Payment timing.** Your template at cell B16 makes payment conditional on all project funding being
confirmed. We have scoped this project so it has no other funder. The risk is removed at the design stage.

**Young people's safety.** Our own child safety process is in draft. Until it is complete, this work
runs inside the policies and screening of the community organisations we deliver with.

**Overstating outcomes.** We do not claim a health outcome from a bed. We count a person off the
floor, a paid hour and a kilogram of plastic, and we publish the method.
