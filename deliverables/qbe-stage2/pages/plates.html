Three Plates for the Deck

Three questions a reader hits that the deck currently answers in prose or not at all. Each plate is drawn at slide proportions with the copy that goes beside it and the frame it belongs to. None is built in Pencil yet.

Plate 1

Can you actually make 400?

new, before S17

On the slide

The press is the slowest machine.

Everything else waits for it, so the line runs at three beds a day and 48 a month. The 400 takes 8.3 months.

Two things lift it to 80 a month and five months. Buy panels from Defy at $40,712 across the run, or buy a second press once at $22,500. Doing both buys nothing, because assembly caps the line at five a day either way.

Why this plate

S17 already claims 48 beds a month and nothing shows why. This is the slide that survives the question a manufacturer on the panel will ask.

It also turns the second press into a visible decision. The two lifts cost the same across the 400 when shred lands at $3.16 a kilogram, and the quotes that carry that price have not been opened.

Do not print the $3.16 on the slide. It invites a question we cannot answer yet.

Plate 2

Where does the money stop?

COJmo · S13 The model

On the slide

Two kinds of money that never meet.

Catalytic capital pays Goods $750 for a community's first stock. Buyers pay the community enterprise, and that money never reaches Goods.

The enterprise keeps the whole sale. A hundred beds is $75,000 of local capital and it funds whatever the community decides next.

Why this plate

S13 already carries this sentence in its footer, and draws the model as five numbered steps. A numbered list reads as a process. This is a structure.

The red cross is the point. A funder looking at one loop assumes Goods is repaid out of community sales and asks why the grant is needed again next year. Two loops answers that before it is asked, and the answer is that Goods is carried by its own trade.

Plate 3

How do you know anyone wants them?

LX4ci · S12, beside the paid trade

On the slide

We do not have a demand number.

We have a list of who asked and a separate list of who paid. Four organisations have already paid for 320 beds, including a research centre that paid for a hundred up front before one was made.

Ordered by what people did, the largest figure sits on the weakest rung.

Why this plate

Every applicant in the room will show a demand pipeline. Saying we do not have one, and showing why the number would be meaningless, is the strongest thing on the page.

It also protects the application. The 778 that used to be quoted adds a figure scoped to one Elder's wallet to one scoped to the children of an outstation. An assessor who reads the register finds that in a minute.

Figures from the guarded modules: production-scenarios, the-year-and-the-raise, who-has-asked and demand-and-buyers. Nothing here is built in Pencil. Each plate names the frame it belongs to, and slides get built one at a time on Ben's word.
