Three Plates for the Deck

Three questions a reader hits that the deck currently answers in prose or not at all. Each plate is drawn at slide proportions with the copy that goes beside it and the frame it belongs to. None is built in Pencil yet.

Plate 1

Can you actually make 400?

new, before S17

On the slide

The tab press is the slowest machine.

It presses six tab sheets a day, one to a kit, so the line dispatches 96 flat-packed kits a month and the 400 take 4.17 months. Legs are bought and assembly happens in community, so neither one caps the factory.

One lift is on the table. A second tab press at $22,500 takes the line to the router ceiling of 136 kits a month and the 400 to 2.9 months. That ceiling is the router's entered rate, and it still needs bought legs, shred, staff and packing behind it. More bought leg stock can cover a shortage and never adds a kit.

Why this plate

S17 claims a monthly rate and nothing on the slide shows where it comes from. This is the slide that survives the question a manufacturer on the panel will ask.

It also keeps the second press an honest decision. What a set of bought legs costs a kit is still open. It turns on how many leg sets come out of one 800 by 1200 panel, which is Nic's to confirm, and on the bag price in quotes QU0494 and QU0495, neither of which has been opened.

Print no plastic cost on this slide. The old panel bill across the 400 and the shred break-even a kilogram were both worked out on the route Ben retired on 12 September. Both are withdrawn with it and neither has a replacement yet.

Plate 2

Where does the money stop?

COJmo · S13 The model

On the slide

Two kinds of money that never meet.

Catalytic capital pays Goods $750 for a community's first stock. Buyers pay the community enterprise, and that money never reaches Goods.

The enterprise keeps the whole sale. A hundred beds sold at $750 is $75,000 of local capital, less when beds are given, and it funds whatever the community decides next.

Why this plate

S13 already carries this sentence in its footer, and draws the model as five numbered steps. A numbered list reads as a process. This is a structure.

The red cross is the point. A funder looking at one loop assumes Goods is repaid out of community sales and asks why the grant is needed again next year. Two loops answers that before it is asked, and the answer is that Goods is carried by its own trade, at a provisional 628 paid beds a year.

The making figures on the Goods side are provisional and the slide says so. The route changed on 12 September and what a bought leg set costs is not settled, so the provisional $276 and $474 hold until Nic confirms the panel yield.

Plate 3

How do you know anyone wants them?

LX4ci · S12, beside the paid trade

On the slide

We do not have a demand number.

We have a list of who asked and a separate list of who paid. Four organisations have already paid for 320 beds, including a research centre that paid for a hundred up front before one was made.

Ordered by what people did, the largest figure sits on the weakest rung.

Why this plate

Every applicant in the room will show a demand pipeline. Saying we do not have one, and showing why the number would be meaningless, is the strongest thing on the page.

It also protects the application. The 778 that used to be quoted adds a figure scoped to one Elder's wallet to one scoped to the children of an outstation. An assessor who reads the register finds that in a minute.

Figures from the guarded modules: production-route, production-scenarios, the-year-and-the-raise, who-has-asked and demand-and-buyers. Nothing here is built in Pencil. Each plate names the frame it belongs to, and slides get built one at a time on Ben's word.
