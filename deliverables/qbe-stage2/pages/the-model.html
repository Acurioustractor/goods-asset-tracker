The Model

Buying something a community already wants is how a community-owned factory gets started.

Three drawings and one sentence. The money, the migration and the measures. Every figure comes from a guarded module, so this page and the spreadsheet cannot drift apart.

One. Where the money stops

Two loops, and they never join. This is the drawing that answers the question a funder asks in year two.

The gold arrow is the only crossing and it carries beds. The red cross marks the arrow a funder expects to find and will not. Goods is carried by the left loop.

Two. How the making moves

Witta carries the first beds. The plants take over as the need grows, and Witta changes job. This is what the $300,000 is for.

Bed counts are targets that sit inside measured capacity. Witta makes 576 a year at three beds a day. A new plant makes about 200 in its first year and reaches 720 on the same press.

What $300,000 proves in twelve months

That making can move to a community without stranding the equipment that got it started. Witta does not become a spare factory. It becomes the parts supplier for the places now doing the assembly.

The sequence matters and it is the opposite of the usual one. The demand is already there before the plant is. Palm Island has 131 beds deployed and three organisations asking. Maningrida has 58 beds, eight washing machines and two organisations that have paid. The plant follows the relationship home.

Three. The capital model

Where every dollar goes and what job it does. The arrow in the middle is the one nobody draws, and it is why the gap is smaller than the operating hole.

Bed money is asked at the $750 sale price and the making line is the $276 cost, so $160,000 of bed money over-covers its own line by $49,600. That surplus is the $474 carrying the organisation. The operating line is short $197,550 and the gap is $147,950, and the difference between those two is exactly that arrow.

Four. The enterprise model

What one community pool does. The arithmetic at the end is the catalytic argument, and it is a coincidence nobody designed.

Four pools of 100 beds at $750 is $300,000. Two community plants at the $150,000 allowance is $300,000. Nobody designed those to match, which is what makes it worth saying. It is a statement of scale and not a claim that the money doubles: the beds are funded by Brian M. Davis, Snow and the gap, and what a community does with its own sales is its decision.

Five. The impact dashboard

Five measures. One is counted, two are modelled, one has no trade behind it yet, and one carries no number at all.

Money kept in community sits behind procurement shifted and is the same event seen from the community side. It is a settled rule, the full $750, with no enterprise trading against it yet.

We do not have a demand number and do not claim one

Every bed figure on the record is somebody's impression of a population nobody has counted. We present what people did instead: money moved, money named, an organisation asked, a person asked, it came up in a meeting. The largest figure we hold sits on the weakest rung.

The plainest thing we can say needs no model at all. Beds have gone to eleven communities and not one has come back to say it has enough.
