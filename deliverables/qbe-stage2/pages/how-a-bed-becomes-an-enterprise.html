PLATE 02

What a buyer actually pays for

Five invoices

A bed is $750. That is a set price, not one we discover order by order. What changes between invoices is everything else on them, and that turns out to be the interesting part.

Buyers have already paid $50,000 for community-build and program-support visits, across ten of them billed between $3,000 and $8,000. That is the same work the facilitation line in our ask pays for. It is not an overhead we invented: it is a line buyers have been paying alongside the beds since the first order.

Two kinds of money that never meet

The one thing to get right

PLATE 05

Two plants, and why those two

The ask

QBE is asked for $300,000 to build two community plants at $150,000 each. A plant is a fixed list: a site base, collection and sorting, shredding, pressing with CNC and finishing, and assembly. About three months from money to first bed.

Plant onePalm Island The council, the community and PICC have each asked for a facility. 131 beds are already there, third of eleven communities. Seven people are cleared to speak for it, more than anywhere else. $150,000 · about 200 beds in year one
Plant twoManingrida Forty beds were pressed at our facility and assembled at Gamardi by young people with Homeland School Company. The making has already travelled there once. $150,000 · first beds by mid-2027

Neither is chosen on how many beds are recorded as wanted. They are chosen because a community asked, someone local can run it, and beds have already landed there. Alice Springs asked too, and is funded separately through Oonchiumpa, so it sits outside this request.

Allowance, not a quote$150,000 sits above the priced module range of $95,767 to $142,467 and below the $207,450 it costs to hand a site over running. Nine of the thirteen modules carry estimates. A site letter and a quote basis for each plant are owed before this is defended.

Freeing a cooking slot by buying costs about $162 a bed. One Defy panel is $200 and does the legs for one bed, so you buy the leg sheet and still cook the tab sheet. That is about $227 of plastic a bed against about $65 of shred when both sheets are cooked here. The panel route buys capacity at roughly $162 a bed, every bed, for as long as you use it. The second press buys the same capacity once, for about $22,500, and then costs nothing a bed.

What a bought panel replacesOne panel does the legs for one bed. So buying frees the leg slot and the tab sheet is still cooked here, which is why six sheets a day becomes six beds and not twelve. The Defy invoice record describes the same panel as a bed’s worth of parts, which would free both slots; that reading is not used here. Open Item 20 in the sheet still has the route unmodelled, so Nic confirms the order quantities and the timing.

Getting the 400 finished by winter

First stock is ordered once the boards sit, so the line starts in December with about 43 beds of parts left over after ALIVE’s hundred. From there it is a straight ramp, and the only question is how many sheets a day it runs at.

What has to be true for the green lineThe second press has to be running by March, which means Nic prices it on Monday and it is ordered before Christmas. The Defy record’s own conclusion is to buy the press and not the panels, unless the press cannot be running by mid October. Everything on this chart also assumes the boards land in November and first stock is ordered in December.

What that means for the beds already spoken for

630 beds are recorded against Witta: ALIVE’s 100 from November, 400 of first stock, and Centrecorp’s 130 from December. That is 1,260 cooked sheets if everything is made here, and about 21 bulka bags of shred.

The plants do not make this year’s bedsNeither community plant has a start date and no part of the 630 is allocated to one. Every bed committed for the next twelve months comes off the line at Witta, which is why the second press and the QBE request are separate decisions that do not substitute for each other.

So when is a community plant needed? Not to clear this queue. A plant is needed when a community wants the making itself, and the test is the same three things every time: they have asked, someone local can run it, and beds have already landed there. A plant starts at about 200 beds in its first year and reaches 720 on the same six sheets a day as the local crew builds speed.

PLATE 11

Questions people actually ask

Nine
Does the QBE money buy beds?

No. It builds two plants. The beds are paid for separately by the Tim Fairfax Family Foundation, the Brian M. Davis Charitable Foundation and an ask to Snow, so a community’s plant and a community’s stock are never competing for the same dollar.

What happens if you only get $150,000?

One plant instead of two. It goes to Palm Island. Nothing else about the model changes, because a plant is a fixed list of modules and not a variable. Maningrida waits and keeps taking beds pressed at our facility, which is how the forty beds at Gamardi were made.

Who owns the plant?

The intention is that it ends up community-owned, and that is a pathway, still being walked. The month-six test asks the ownership questions out loud: who holds the keys, who runs the payroll, who invoices the buyer, and whether at least half the production is local.

What does the community actually keep?

The full price of any bed it sells. The whole thing. That is more than Goods keeps on a bed it sells itself, which is $474. Those proceeds are the community’s and they cannot be used to repay anything Goods borrows.

How many beds have you actually sold?

320, across five invoices to four organisations, for $273,966. Separately the register records 540 beds deployed across eleven communities, which counts a different thing: beds that reached a community, including prototypes and gifted beds. We never add the two.

Is the plastic claim measured?

20 kg is the design mass of a finished bed, so it is modelled. About 36 kg of shred goes through the press for one bed and roughly 16 kg of melt edge and router offcut goes back to the shredder. It is not measured landfill diversion and we do not present it as one.

What is your biggest risk?

Concentration. One foundation was more than half of last year’s Goods receipts and there is no ask that replaces it. That is a conversation to have, and it is worth more than any single ask on the table.

Why does freight keep coming up?

Because it is real money and it is not the same to every place. We charged Homeland $5,900 to move forty beds from Brisbane through Darwin to Maningrida. We quoted Mala’la $3,200 to move thirteen to the same town and then carried it ourselves. Freight per bed falls sharply with volume, which is one of the reasons making locally matters.

What is not settled yet?

Nothing in the funding is signed. Twelve cost lines in the plan are still unpriced. The opening cash position is unknown and comes with the applicant’s cashflow. We would rather name those than imply they are handled.
