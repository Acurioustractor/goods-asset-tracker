PLATE 02

What a buyer actually pays for

Five invoices

A bed is $750. That is a set price, not one we discover order by order. What changes between invoices is everything else on them, and that turns out to be the interesting part.

Buyers have already paid $50,000 for community-build and program-support visits, across ten of them billed between $3,000 and $8,000. That is the same work the facilitation line in our ask pays for. It is not an overhead we invented: it is a line buyers have been paying alongside the beds since the first order.

PLATE 03

Two kinds of money that never meet

The one thing to get right

This is the part people get wrong, so it is worth being blunt about. Philanthropy pays Goods for beds. Buyers pay the community. The two never touch.

PLATE 04

What a bed costs, and what a year costs

Two separate things

A bed sells for $750. The making allowance is a provisional $276, so a provisional $474 stays with Goods. That allowance was priced when both sheets were pressed at Witta. On the ruled route the tab sheet is pressed here and the leg panel is bought, so the allowance is carried forward and not yet re-costed. Running the organisation is a separate figure, paid for by everything Goods sells, and the two are never divided into each other.

What a year costs, in a year’s money

Running the whole organisation is $297,550 a year. Five lines, and none of them is per bed.

Claim ceilingThe provisional $276 is modelled, has not been measured, and was priced for the old route where both sheets were pressed at Witta. The $80 wage line inside it sits alongside the ruled two hours of paid making a bed. What the ruled route costs turns on the yield of a bought leg panel, which Nic owns and which is not settled. It holds until the first fifty kits come off a line at working pace and are timed and weighed, which is one of the things the two plants exist to produce.
PLATE 05

Two plants, and why those two

The ask

QBE is asked for $300,000 to build two community plants at $150,000 each. A plant is a fixed list: a site base, collection and sorting, shredding, pressing with CNC and finishing, and assembly. About three months from money to first bed.

Plant onePalm Island The council, the community and PICC have each asked for a facility. 131 beds are already there, third of eleven communities. Seven people are cleared to speak for it, more than anywhere else. $150,000 · about 200 beds in year one
Plant twoManingrida Forty beds were pressed at our facility and assembled at Gamardi by young people with Homeland School Company. The making has already travelled there once. $150,000 · first beds by mid-2027

Neither is chosen on how many beds are recorded as wanted. They are chosen because a community asked, someone local can run it, and beds have already landed there. Alice Springs asked too, and is funded separately through Oonchiumpa, so it sits outside this request.

Six sheets a day is the facility

The one number

Everything about what Witta can make comes back to one thing. The press cooks six tab sheets a day. On the ruled route that is six kits a day, because a kit takes one pressed tab sheet and the leg panel is bought in. The factory presses, routs, packs and dispatches, and the job ends there. Assembly happens in community, so nothing in the building is waiting on it.

Buying the leg panel is the ruled route, and it moves the cost of a bed onto a number we do not have. The invoiced 800 by 1200 panel is $127.23 before GST and the legs are cut from it here. How many leg kits one panel yields is not settled, so the plastic in a kit cannot be stated. Buying the leg kit finished from Defy instead, excluding tabs, is $344.05. Until the yield lands, the $276 making allowance and the $474 that stays with Goods are both provisional.

The open number, and who owns itThe yield of a bought panel is Nic’s to settle, and the order quantities and the timing go with it. The shred break-even that used to sit here was computed for the route where both sheets were pressed at Witta, so it is withdrawn and not replaced. A second press is still about $22,500 and still worth pricing, because it lifts the month from 96 to 136, where the router caps it.

Getting the 400 made

First stock is ordered once the boards sit, so the line starts in December. There are 81 kits of legs already cut and ALIVE’s hundred uses all of them, so the 400 starts from nothing. At 96 kits a month that is 4.17 months and 6.0 tonnes of tab shred.

Even then the router holds the month at 136, so a second press buys about five weeks on the 400 and not a doubling. Everything here also assumes the boards land in November and first stock is ordered in December.

The plants do not make this year’s bedsNeither community plant has a start date and no part of the 500 is allocated to one. Every bed committed for the next twelve months comes off the line at Witta, which is why the second press and the QBE request are separate decisions that do not substitute for each other.

So when is a community plant needed? Not to clear this queue. A plant is needed when a community wants the making itself, and the test is the same three things every time: they have asked, someone local can run it, and beds have already landed there. A plant starts at about 200 beds in its first year and reaches 720 as the local crew builds speed.

PLATE 11

Questions people actually ask

Nine
Does the QBE money buy beds?

No. It builds two plants. The beds are paid for separately by the Brian M. Davis Charitable Foundation and an ask to Snow, with 187 still to find, so a community’s plant and a community’s stock are never competing for the same dollar.

What happens if you only get $150,000?

One plant instead of two. It goes to Palm Island. Nothing else about the model changes, because a plant is a fixed list of modules and not a variable. Maningrida waits and keeps taking beds pressed at our facility, which is how the forty beds at Gamardi were made.

Who owns the plant?

The intention is that it ends up community-owned, and that is a pathway, still being walked. The month-six test asks the ownership questions out loud: who holds the keys, who runs the payroll, who invoices the buyer, and whether at least half the production is local.

What does the community actually keep?

The full price of any bed it sells. The whole thing. That is more than Goods keeps on a bed it sells itself, which is a provisional $474. Those proceeds are the community’s and they cannot be used to repay anything Goods borrows.

How many beds have you actually sold?

320, across five invoices to four organisations, for $273,966. Separately the register records 540 beds deployed across eleven communities, which counts a different thing: beds that reached a community, including prototypes and gifted beds. We never add the two.

Is the plastic claim measured?

20 kg is the design mass of a finished bed, so it is modelled. On the ruled route 15 kg of that is the tab sheet pressed at Witta and the leg panel is bought already pressed. It is not measured landfill diversion and we do not present it as one.

What is your biggest risk?

Concentration. One foundation was more than half of last year’s Goods receipts and there is no ask that replaces it. That is a conversation to have, and it is worth more than any single ask on the table.

Why does freight keep coming up?

Because it is real money and it is not the same to every place. We charged Homeland $5,900 to move forty beds from Brisbane through Darwin to Maningrida. We quoted Mala’la $3,200 to move thirteen to the same town and then carried it ourselves. Freight per bed falls sharply with volume, which is one of the reasons making locally matters.

What is not settled yet?

Nothing in the funding is signed. What a kit costs is provisional, because nobody has yet measured how many leg kits come out of one bought panel. Twelve cost lines in the plan are still unpriced. The opening cash position is unknown and comes with the applicant’s cashflow. We would rather name those than imply they are handled.

Every figure here traces to a source that can be checked: Xero for the trade, the asset register for deployment, the model workbook for the costs, and the storyteller registry for consent on any quoted voice.
