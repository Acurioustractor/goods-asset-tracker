## Organisation history, vision and mission (max 250 words)
Goods on Country makes things communities have asked for, in the communities that asked. It is a registered business name of The Butterfly Movement Ltd, a charity. Its directors are Kristy Bloomfield, Audrey Deemal and Sonia Mascolo. Jeremy Donovan stands for election at the AGM on 12 October 2026. The founders, Ben Knight and Nicholas Marchesi, are employed by the charity to run Goods on Country.

The work started with a bed. In remote communities, families sleep on floors because a bed cannot be bought locally and freight costs more than the bed. We designed a bed with communities that answers that. The Stretch Bed sits off the ground, the canvas comes off and washes, every part can be replaced, and the legs are pressed from about 20 kg of recycled plastic. It assembles in about five minutes with no tools.

540 beds are recorded in homes across eleven communities. 320 have been bought and paid for by four organisations, $247,770 net of GST, including 100 paid up front by the ALIVE National Centre at the University of Melbourne in August 2026. Forty were pressed at our own facility in Queensland and assembled in Maningrida by local young workers.

The vision is that the making, the selling and the money stay in community. Our model puts first trading stock into the hands of a community enterprise, connects it to buyers who already hold health and housing budgets, and moves the plant itself into community hands once the enterprise can run it. The enterprise sells the beds and keeps the full price.

We do not claim a health outcome. We count a person off the floor, a paid hour of work and a kilogram of plastic kept out of landfill, each against a record.

## Primary beneficiaries (max 150 words)
Young people in remote Aboriginal communities who want paid work where they live, and the households who receive the beds they make and deliver.

The young people are the reason this application exists. In Maningrida, local young workers assembled forty beds at Gamardi. In Alice Springs and Utopia, young people working with an aged care service made more than twenty beds for Elders. Karen Liddle, who leads that work, put it this way: "To see kids' faces with joy after making a bed, it just really hits you." The households are the second beneficiary. They are families in very remote communities where overcrowding runs at 49% in Central Australia and 70% in Arnhem Land, against 15% nationally.

## Initiative description (max 300 words)
$99,750 buys 133 Stretch Beds at $750, the published price every buyer pays. The beds become the first trading stock of community enterprises in four communities, about 33 each. The enterprise sells them to buyers who already hold health and housing budgets, and keeps the money. Nothing returns to Goods. A community enterprise with stock has something to sell and something to make more of.

The work around the beds is inside that price. The trips, the build days, the training, the delivery and the slow work of a local organisation deciding how it wants to run are all carried by Goods on Country out of its share of the $750. No separate facilitation or freight line is asked for.

Young people are paid to assemble and deliver. Kits are pressed and cut at our facility in Queensland and dispatched flat packed. Assembly happens in community, by young people, with the community organisation setting roles, hours and rates. On our modelled rate of about two hours of paid work a bed, 133 beds is about 266 paid hours in community. That rate is modelled from the build and will be measured on this project in the production log we opened this month.

The communities are ones we already work in, where beds have already arrived and relationships already exist. Palm Island, Maningrida, Utopia and Tennant Creek are the working choices. No community is named in a funding application before it has agreed, so the final four are confirmed with each organisation before the grant starts.

Each bed carries about 20 kg of recycled plastic by design. Across 133 beds that is about 2.7 tonnes kept out of landfill, in places that sit outside kerbside collection entirely. The plastic will be weighed on this project. The 20 kg is a design figure until it is.

## Why is this initiative needed (max 300 words)
A child sleeping on a floor in an overcrowded house is where the chain that ends in rheumatic heart disease begins. Skin infection leads to acute rheumatic fever, and repeated episodes cause rheumatic heart disease. The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare names household overcrowding as the risk factor and defines it by the Canadian National Occupancy Standard.

Rheumatic heart disease among First Nations people in the Northern Territory runs at 3,398.7 per 100,000. In 2024 there were 506 new acute rheumatic fever diagnoses in Australia, 93% of them First Nations, and 211 of them in children aged five to fourteen. Overcrowding by that same standard runs at 70% in Arnhem Land and 49% in Central Australia against 15% nationally.

You cannot buy a bed in these communities. One provider in Alice Springs sells $3 million a year of washing machines into remote communities, and most of them are in the dump within months. Daniel Patrick Noble, on Palm Island, described why: "the cost of, not only purchasing it, the cost of having it freighted over as well, it all adds." Mark, also on Palm Island, described what people do instead: "We put together crates, tied them up with plastic, joined them together to make it look like a bed."

The second need is work. In very remote Australia 35% of First Nations people aged 25 to 64 are employed, against 62% in major cities. Young people leave because there is nothing to stay for.

The third is waste. 87% of Australia's plastic goes straight to landfill, and remote communities sit outside kerbside collection altogether. The same plastic that has nowhere to go is the material the bed is made from.

We do not claim the bed changes the disease figures. We claim a person off the floor, a paid hour and a kilogram of plastic, each counted against a record.

## Key risks and mitigations (max 250 words)
A community has not agreed. We will not name a community in a grant application before it has agreed to the work. Four are our working choices and each confirms before the grant starts. If one declines, the stock moves to another community where beds have already been delivered.

The making bottleneck. Our press makes six kits a day and assembly happens in community, so 133 kits is about 22 press days, inside two months on a sixteen-day working month. No new equipment is needed for this project.

Freight. Remote freight is about $100 a bed all up, measured on a real forty-bed run to Maningrida. The organisation absorbs it out of its share of the price. It does not come out of the wage and it is not asked of you.

Payment timing. Your template at cell B16 makes payment conditional on all project funding being confirmed. This project has no other funder, so the risk is removed at the design stage.

Young people's safety. Our own child safety process is in draft. Until it is complete, this work runs inside the policies and screening of the community organisations we deliver with.

Overstating outcomes. We do not claim a health outcome from a bed. We count a person off the floor, a paid hour and a kilogram of plastic, and we publish the method.
