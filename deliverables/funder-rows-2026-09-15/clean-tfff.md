## 3.7 How will TFFF funding strengthen delivery of your Strategic Plan? (200 words)

A three-year grant that buys 133 beds a year is the resilience you named, because the same beds do a different job each year as trade grows underneath them. Running Goods on Country costs $251,224 a year. Of every $750 bed, $288 reaches the organisation once making, freight and facilitation are paid. At 874 paid beds a year the organisation carries itself with no grant at all.

Year one, to June 2027, plans 400 beds of first stock. Your 133 are a third of them, and their contribution is a third of what the organisation costs. Year two plans 874 beds, the break-even. Your 133 are stock for the two new community plants, and their contribution is the reserve that lets a bad quarter happen without a redundancy. Year three plans 900 beds. Your 133 are the stock a community starts with when it takes its plant over.

Bed volumes are targets inside modelled capacity. No buyer has ordered years two or three, and nothing in this raise is secured.

## 3.8 Provide two examples of your organisation's impact over the last three years. (300 words)

Maningrida, May 2026. The Homeland School Company bought 40 Stretch Beds. We pressed and cut the parts at our facility in Queensland and sent them north flat packed. At Gamardi, young people assembled all 40 with the school and handed them out. That run is the route we now use everywhere: make the parts centrally, assemble in community, pay local people for the assembly. Two organisations in that one community have now bought from us. Mala'la Health Service bought 13 beds in October 2025. Eight washing machines are in community there as well.

Utopia, August 2025 to May 2026. Centrecorp Foundation bought 167 beds across two orders for the Utopia homelands. Oonchiumpa held the build and Urapuntja staff led the local delivery. 147 of those beds are in households today and every one traces to a batch in our register. Young people working with the aged care service made more than twenty beds for Elders. Karen Liddle, who leads that work, said: "To see kids' faces with joy after making a bed, it just really hits you." Mykel, who built beds on the May trip, slept on his that night and said he would be back every day to make them.

Both show the organisation working the way the plan says it should. A community organisation buys, local people make and deliver, and the record holds. 540 beds are recorded across eleven communities. 320 are bought and paid for, $247,770 net of GST. We count beds in households and paid hours of local work.

## 4.5 Describe how your board and executive resolved an unexpected governance issue. (200 words)

In June 2026 the Butterfly board of four resigned together to make way for Goods on Country, leaving one transitional director and putting the three-director minimum at risk. The board filled two casual vacancies with Kristy Bloomfield and Audrey Deemal and kept Sonia Mascolo as transitional director. It set the FY26 audit and handover with the accountants, and called the AGM for 12 October 2026 so the incoming board is elected by members under the constitution's 28-day notice rule. The two founders, Ben Knight and Nic Marchesi, are employees of the charity. Neither sits on the board, so the board stays independent of the people who run the work.

## 5.1 Describe up to three key stakeholder or beneficiary groups and how you engage them. (300 words)

Community organisations. Councils, health services, schools and Aboriginal corporations are the buyers, the hosts and the decision-makers. We work with eleven communities and four organisations have bought beds. A community is never named in a funding application before it has agreed. A plant goes only where the organisation has asked in its own words, somebody local wants to run it, and beds are already there.

Young people who make and deliver. In Maningrida, Utopia and Alice Springs young people have assembled and delivered beds for pay. They set the pace of the work and their community organisation sets the roles and rates. The production log we opened this month records their paid hours against completed beds.

Households, and the people who speak for them. Experience is recorded in Empathy Ledger, our consent-based storytelling platform, with consent held per person and per quote. Thirty-four people are cleared to be quoted. Palm Island has seven, more than anywhere else. Nobody is quoted who has not cleared it, and a community's own register of who sleeps where stays with the community.

Oonchiumpa Consultancy and Services, Alice Springs. An Aboriginal community-controlled organisation that holds a Commonwealth offer to run a youth manufacturing pathway. Goods designs and builds the plant. Oonchiumpa runs the program and the people.
