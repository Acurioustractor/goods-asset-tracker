## Purpose or mission (max 900 characters)
Goods on Country makes things communities have asked for, in the communities that asked. Families in remote Aboriginal communities sleep on floors because a bed cannot be bought locally and freight costs more than the bed. We designed a bed with communities that answers that. It sits off the ground, the canvas washes, every part can be replaced and the legs are pressed from 20 kg of recycled plastic. 540 beds are in homes across eleven communities. The charity's job is to put the making, the selling and the money in community hands. Young people are paid to make and deliver, community organisations hold the stock and decide who gets a bed, and the production plant moves to community ownership once the enterprise can run it.

## The specific project (max 1,800 characters)
A twelve-month program in which young people aged 15 to 21 assemble and deliver 66 Stretch Beds to households in their own community, paid at an agreed rate, inside the youth program of the community organisation that holds them. Kits are pressed at our facility in Queensland from recycled plastic and shipped flat. The young people assemble them over supervised build days across the year and deliver them with a mentor, so each bed is theirs from the first bolt to the front door. The community organisation decides which households receive beds and keeps any beds it chooses to sell, with the whole $750 staying local. The community is one we already work in, where beds have already arrived and the organisation has asked in its own words. About 12 to 15 young people take part over the year and 66 households get a bed. The Mazda Foundation grant funds the 66 beds. Mentoring, transport and supervision are carried by the community organisation and by Goods on Country. This fits Pathways for Young People because the work is ongoing and paid, and sits inside a program that already supports these young people through school, court and family transitions.

## The need (max 1,500 characters)
You cannot buy a bed in these communities. Overcrowding by the national occupancy standard runs at 70 per cent in Arnhem Land and 49 per cent in Central Australia, against 15 per cent nationally. Children sleep on floors in crowded houses, and skin infection in those conditions is the recognised pathway to acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease. In 2024 there were 506 new acute rheumatic fever diagnoses in Australia, 93 per cent of them First Nations and 211 of them children aged five to fourteen. Mark, on Palm Island, described what people do instead of a bed: "We put together crates, tied them up with plastic, joined them together to make it look like a bed." The second need is work. In very remote Australia 35 per cent of First Nations people aged 25 to 64 are employed, against 62 per cent in major cities, and young people leave because there is nothing to stay for. The bed is also made from the plastic that remote communities have no way to recycle. 87 per cent of Australia's plastic goes to landfill.

## Experience (max 1,200 characters)
Forty Stretch Beds were pressed at our facility and assembled at Gamardi, Maningrida, in 2026 by young people with Homeland School Company, a community-controlled homeland education organisation. In Alice Springs, Oonchiumpa brought young people in for two days to build beds from flat-pack. Some were paid for the work, sixteen beds stayed in town and the rest went to the Utopia homelands. Karen Liddle, who leads that work, said: "To see kids' faces with joy after making a bed, it just really hits you." 540 beds are recorded in homes across eleven communities, every one against a batch, a community and a date. 320 of them were bought and paid for by four organisations, including 100 paid up front by the ALIVE National Centre at the University of Melbourne.

## Expected outcomes (max 1,500 characters)
66 beds assembled and delivered by young people in their own community, every bed traceable to a household and a date. 12 to 15 young people paid for making and delivery work across the year, with hours logged against completed beds, and at least three moving into ongoing paid roles with the community organisation or with Goods on Country's production work. 1.3 tonnes of plastic kept out of landfill and pressed into a product that stays in use for years. The community organisation holds stock it can sell or give, and makes its first decision on what to do with the proceeds. We do not claim a health outcome from a bed. We count a person off the floor, a paid hour of work and a kilogram of plastic, and we publish the method.

## Evaluation (max 1,200 characters)
Three records already run. The asset register holds every bed with its community, date and batch and is reconciled against invoices. The production log records paid hours against completed beds and the names of the young people paid. The consented story registry records what each person has agreed may be quoted. We propose follow-up with households at delivery, at six weeks and at three months, done by a paid local person with four questions. That collection has not yet been done and we would label it that way in any report. Annual reporting to the Mazda Foundation would carry the register extract, the hours log and the young people's own words where consent is recorded.
