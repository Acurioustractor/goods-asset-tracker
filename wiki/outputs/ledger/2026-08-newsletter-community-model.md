## The newsletter

**The bed is the least interesting part.**

This month we introduced you to four people from four communities. If you missed them, they are worth a minute each: Dianne Stokes in Tennant Creek, the Elder who designed and named the products; Mykel in Alice Springs, building his own bed and wanting the job; Dorrie at Arlparra in the Utopia Homelands, on delivery day; and Jahvan on Palm Island, on breaking the cycle.

Here is the model underneath those four stories, in plain words.

**It started in community, not in a warehouse.** The Stretch Bed and Pakkimjalki Kari, the washing machine, were designed and named in Tennant Creek. Dianne Stokes gave the machine its Warumungu name. Design authority sits in community, and everything else in the model follows from that.

**Because donated goods disappear.** Beds shipped into remote communities have a short life and a long freight bill. Daniel Patrick Noble on Palm Island put it best:

"The cost of, not only purchasing it, the cost of having it freighted over as well, it all adds up... a lot of them are low income earners... And sometimes people would rather just go without."

The same arithmetic holds from the barge to Palm Island to the road trains into the Barkly. It is why the answer is not more freight.

**So communities started making their own.** The Stretch Bed is pressed from the plastic already on Country, 20kg of it per bed. So far the ledger reads 540 beds in communities, 22 washing machines working, and 3,540kg of plastic diverted into furniture that stays.

**Every community chooses differently.** This is the part that makes the model a model. There is no standard package. A community picks only the pieces it wants: products, equipment, a workshop, training, people, systems, the enterprise itself, the money that bridges it, and the story told on its own terms. Urapuntja is talking about one shredder. Tennant Creek is talking about one small activity through the existing Shed. Oonchiumpa has asked for the whole production pathway. Palm Island has asked us to start by listening.

**And ownership is the destination, not the pitch.** We hold ourselves to a test with four checkpoints: who holds the keys, who is on payroll, who invoices the buyer, who decides what gets made. No site has passed it yet, because no site has entered production for sale yet. We would rather tell you that plainly than claim a handover that has not happened. When the first site becomes testable, you will read it here first.

Jahvan is why the test exists:

"There's gotta be a break in the cycle somewhere. Soon as you break the cycle, all this stuff stop."

He is training to run production himself. The model is the machinery for getting him, and people like him in every community that wants it, to the other side of that sentence.
