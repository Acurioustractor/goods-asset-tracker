Margaret has lived on her homeland for about thirty years — one of the outstations of the
Utopia (Urapuntja) region, Alyawarr country.

When our truck arrived she was sleeping in a tent. Her words, not mine:

"I sleep in the tent but look, there's my house or the rubbish house... No mattress. Look,
the bed was mine. Just for chucking us blanket, that's all, that's all I was sleeping."

"Because I was sleeping on floor, turn around, turn around. Yeah. No comfortable nothing."

The bed went together in front of her. No tools, about five minutes. Canvas she can sleep
straight on — out here mattresses go missing, get wet, can't be washed. Then her verdict,
in full:

"Our bed is right. That's good. Good... I can sleep there. Yeah."

Our bed. She approved it and owned it in the same breath.

Soon we head back to Utopia for the third time. Each trip the relationship gets more
solid, and the question has changed. It's no longer can we get beds onto Country. It's
what it takes to build them on Country, with the people who sleep on them.

Margaret's 292 words live in the Empathy Ledger under her ongoing consent — her story
stays hers, the same way the bed does. That's the whole model.
